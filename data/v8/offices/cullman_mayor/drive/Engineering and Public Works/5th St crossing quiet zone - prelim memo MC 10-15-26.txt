Re: Train horn quiet zone at the 5th Street crossing - what it would take (preliminary)

You asked at the October 12 department heads meeting for a cost estimate on a quiet zone at the 5th Street crossing of the CSX line, after the calls and the public comment at Council. This is a first look so you can answer questions. It is not a design and the numbers are planning-level only.

1. WHY THE HORNS BLOW

Federal rules require locomotive engineers to sound the horn at every public crossing: 15 to 20 seconds before the crossing, in a long-long-short-long pattern, at any hour. The railroad has no discretion; neither does the City. Night trains are part of normal freight operations on this line. The only way to stop routine horn use at a public crossing is a federally recognized quiet zone.

2. WHAT A QUIET ZONE IS

A quiet zone is a stretch of track at least one-half mile long where the horn is not routinely sounded because the crossings have extra safety features. The City (not the railroad) applies, pays for the improvements, and maintains them. Engineers still sound the horn in an emergency or if someone is on the tracks, and in work zones.

A half mile centered on 5th Street would take in the 5th Street crossing and the two adjacent public crossings. We cannot do 5th Street alone.

3. THE STEPS

a. Notice of intent to the railroad, the state DOT rail section and the Federal Railroad Administration. 60-day comment period.
b. Diagnostic review at each crossing with CSX, the state and the City. This sets what each crossing needs.
c. Design and construction of the safety measures (see below), with CSX doing any work on its own signals and gates under a reimbursement agreement.
d. Updated crossing inventory forms; liability insurance review.
e. Notice of establishment. Quiet takes effect no sooner than 21 days after that notice.

4. WHAT THE CROSSINGS WOULD PROBABLY NEED

All three crossings already have gates and flashing lights. The usual options:
- Raised medians (100 feet each side of the tracks, 60 feet if a driveway is in the way) so drivers can't go around the gates. Cheapest option where the street is wide enough. 5th Street is probably wide enough; one of the adjacent crossings has two driveways close to the tracks.
- Four-quadrant gates (gates on both sides of the road in each direction). Effective, but expensive and CSX installs them.
- Closing a crossing. Not recommended; it would hurt traffic circulation downtown and the Police and Fire Rescue response routes.

5. PLANNING-LEVEL COST

Medians at two crossings, four-quadrant gates at one     $340,000 - $520,000
Engineering, diagnostic review, CSX agreement costs        $60,000 - $90,000
Annual maintenance (City's share, after construction)      $8,000 - $15,000 per year

There is no money for this in the FY2027 budget. It would need a Council appropriation, probably from the capital contingency, or a grant. I am checking whether any state or federal crossing safety program will pay for quiet zone improvements; most will pay for safety upgrades but not for quiet zones as such.

6. HOW LONG

From a Council decision to quiet: 18 to 30 months is typical for a city our size. The railroad's schedule drives most of it. Cities that have gone through it say the diagnostic meeting and the railroad agreement are the slow parts.

7. RECOMMENDED NEXT STEPS

1) Engineering requests an informal meeting with CSX's public projects office and the state rail section in November (no cost, no commitment).
2) I bring a short presentation to a Council work session in January with better numbers.
3) If the Council wants to proceed, budget for the study and design in a mid-year amendment.

7a. THINGS PEOPLE WILL ASK

- "Can the City just ask CSX to stop blowing the horn at night?" No. The horn rule is federal; the railroad has no say in it.
- "Can the City put up a sign?" A "No Train Horn" sign is only posted after a quiet zone is established.
- "What about a wayside horn?" A wayside horn is a stationary horn mounted at the crossing and aimed down the street, used in place of the train's horn. It is quieter for the neighborhood, costs roughly $90,000-$150,000 per crossing, and still needs federal approval. Worth pricing at the same time as the quiet zone.
- "Would a quiet zone make the crossing less safe?" Not if it's done right; the improvements are designed to offset the loss of the horn. The City takes on some added liability and maintenance, which the city attorney should review.
- "How many trains?" The federal crossing inventory for 5th Street (pulled Oct 13 for a records request) lists the railroad's reported daily train count. I'll confirm it with CSX in November; the inventory numbers are often out of date.

8. WHAT TO TELL RESIDENTS NOW

Suggested: "The City is looking seriously at a quiet zone for the 5th Street crossing. It isn't a quick fix: federal rules require safety improvements at the crossing and the ones next to it, and the process with the railroad usually takes a year and a half to two and a half years. The City Engineer will report to the Council this winter with costs. Until a quiet zone is in place, train crews are required by federal law to sound their horns at every crossing, day and night."
