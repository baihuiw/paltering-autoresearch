# Neighborhood Traffic Calming - How Requests Are Handled

City of Cullman Engineering Department, with the Police Department. Revised March 2023. Applies to residential streets inside the city limits. Questions: Engineering, (256) 734-2150, ext. 230.

## Why there is a procedure

Almost every street in Cullman has at least one neighbor who thinks traffic is too fast. Speed humps and other devices slow everyone, including fire trucks and ambulances, and they move traffic onto the next street over. This procedure makes sure the City uses data, treats every neighborhood the same way, and starts with the least intrusive steps.

## Step 1 - Request

Any resident may ask, by calling Engineering or Police, writing the Mayor's office, using the website contact form, or speaking at Council. The request is logged by Engineering with the street, block, and the requester's contact information. Engineering sends the requester an acknowledgment within 10 business days explaining these steps.

## Step 2 - Speed trailer and enforcement (Police)

- The Police Traffic Unit places a speed display trailer on the block for about a week. The trailer records the speed of every vehicle it sees. The Traffic Unit sends the summary to Engineering and to the requester.
- If the trailer shows a pattern (for example, morning speeds well over the limit), the Traffic Unit schedules directed enforcement on the street for one to two weeks at those times.
- Trailers are scheduled first-come, first-served; there are two. Expect 2 to 6 weeks for a spot in the rotation, longer in spring when school zones get priority.

## Step 3 - Speed study (Engineering)

If speeds remain high after enforcement, Engineering does a formal study: road tubes for 48 hours on a weekday, recording volume and speed by hour. Engineering calculates the 85th-percentile speed (the speed that 85% of drivers are at or below).

A street qualifies for physical traffic calming when **all** of the following are true:
- It is a local residential street, not an arterial, collector, or primary emergency response route;
- The posted limit is 25 or 30 mph;
- The 85th-percentile speed is at least **8 mph over** the posted limit;
- Daily traffic is between 300 and 3,000 vehicles;
- The street has no more than two travel lanes and a grade of 8% or less.

Studies are scheduled in spring and fall (dry pavement, school in session). Results go to the requester in writing.

## Step 4 - Neighborhood support

If a street qualifies, the requester circulates a City petition. At least **two-thirds of the households** on the affected section (the block face from intersection to intersection, both sides) must sign in support. Engineering provides the petition form and a map of the affected addresses.

## Step 5 - Design and Council approval

- Engineering selects the device: speed cushions (with gaps for fire trucks), a raised crosswalk, a mini-circle, or curb extensions. Speed humps are spaced 300 to 500 feet apart.
- Engineering confirms with Fire Rescue and Sanitation that the design works for their trucks.
- The project goes on a Council agenda. Typical cost: $3,500 to $6,000 per speed cushion with signs and markings.
- Projects are built in priority order (highest speeds and volumes first) as the annual budget allows, usually with the resurfacing program.

## What the City does not do

- Install stop signs to slow traffic. Stop signs are placed only where the state traffic manual's warrants are met; unwarranted stops are routinely run and can make a street less safe.
- Install "Children at Play" signs. They are not recognized traffic control devices and studies show they don't change speeds.
- Lower a speed limit below 25 mph on a residential street.

## Timeline

From first call to a decision on physical devices, most requests take 3 to 6 months. Enforcement and the speed trailer come first and usually within the first month or two.

## What residents can do right away

- Ask neighbors to slow down. Most speeders on residential streets live within a few blocks.
- Report specific times and patterns ("7:15-7:45 a.m. school days, mostly southbound") rather than "all the time." It helps the Traffic Unit schedule enforcement.
- Keep shrubs and trees trimmed back from corners and stop signs so drivers and children can see each other.
- Use the school bus stop safely: children wait back from the curb, and drivers must stop for a school bus with its stop arm out on a two-lane street in either direction.
