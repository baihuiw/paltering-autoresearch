Dear Mr. Pettus,

Thank you for your letter about the train horns at the 5th Street crossing. You are not the only one; I heard from five people about it at Coffee with the Mayor yesterday, and it came up during public comments at the October 12 Council meeting. I'm sorry for the lost sleep.

You asked why the trains have to blow their horns in the middle of the night, and whether the City can make them stop.

The short answer is that federal law requires train crews to sound the horn at every public crossing, day and night, starting 15 to 20 seconds before the crossing. Neither the railroad nor the City can change that at a single crossing. The one way to stop routine horn use is to create what the federal government calls a "quiet zone."

I asked our City Engineer, Matt Coggins, to look at what a quiet zone at 5th Street would take, and he gave me his first look this week. Here is what I learned:

- A quiet zone has to be at least a half mile long, so it would include the 5th Street crossing and the crossings on either side of it.
- Each crossing in the zone needs extra safety features, such as raised medians so drivers can't go around the gates, or additional gates. The City pays for those, and the railroad has to agree to the design.
- His early estimate is roughly $400,000 to $600,000 in total, and the process with the railroad and federal and state agencies usually takes a year and a half to two and a half years once the City decides to go forward.
- There is no money for it in this year's budget. That doesn't mean no; it means the Council would need to decide to fund it.

Next steps: Engineering will meet informally with the railroad and the state rail office in November, and Mr. Coggins will bring the Council better numbers at a work session in January. I'll make sure your letter is in that packet so the Council hears from the people who live with this every night. Work sessions are open to the public, and you'd be welcome.

In the meantime, a few neighbors have told me that a box fan or white-noise machine in the bedroom has helped more than they expected. I know that's not a solution, and I'm not offering it as one. I'm offering it because I know what a few nights without sleep can do.

I won't promise you a quiet zone in a letter, because it depends on cost and the Council's decision. But I will promise that it is being looked at seriously.

Sincerely,

Denise Kreuzer
Mayor, City of Cullman
