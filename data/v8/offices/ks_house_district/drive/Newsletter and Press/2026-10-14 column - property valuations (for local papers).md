# Statehouse Notes - Column #3

**Your valuation notice and what comes next**

If there's one thing I've learned in my first month as your representative, it's that nothing gets a Kansan to pick up a pen faster than a property valuation notice.

More people have written to my office about valuations than about any other subject, by a wide margin. The letters sound alike: the house hasn't changed, the family hasn't changed, the income hasn't changed, but the number went up 15, 20, 25 percent. Many of them come from retirees who have lived in the same house for decades.

I want to explain how this works, what you can do now, and where I stand.

**Who sets your value?** Your county appraiser, not the state. Appraisers are required to value homes at market value, which they estimate from sales of similar homes nearby. When home prices rise, valuations follow. Farm ground is valued differently, based on what the land can produce rather than what it sells for.

**Does a higher value mean a higher tax?** Not automatically. Your tax is your assessed value times the mill levy set by your county, city, school district and other local governments. If values rise and local governments don't lower their mill levies, your bill goes up. Under state law, a local government that wants to collect more than the "revenue neutral rate" has to notify you, hold a hearing and vote on it. Watch for those notices and those hearings. They're your chance to be heard.

**What can you do now?** This year's appeal period for valuation notices has passed for most people, but you can still pay your tax bill under protest when it comes in December, which opens a review of your value. Your county treasurer can explain how. Seniors and people with disabilities should also ask about the state's property tax refund programs through the Kansas Department of Revenue. My office can help you check whether you qualify.

**Where do I stand?** I support a limit on how fast a homeowner's valuation can climb in a single year. A firm cap would likely require amending the Kansas Constitution, which means two-thirds of each chamber and then a vote of the people. I'll vote to put it on the ballot. I also want valuation notices to be clearer, showing what your tax would be at last year's mill levy, so you know whether the increase is coming from the appraiser or from a budget decision.

A legislative committee studied these questions earlier this month and will take them up again later in October. I'm following that work closely and will report back.

In the meantime, keep writing. Your letters help me make the case in Topeka.
